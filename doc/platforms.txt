Platform collision detection
============================

Crossing a platform can be reduced to two independent tests.

* Does the displacement cross the line defined by the endpoints?

* Does the displacement pass between the endpoints of the platform?

Each question can be answered by comparing the signs of particular angles,
which can be computed using vector algebra identities.

.. image:: asy/cross_platform.svg
   :align: center
   :width: 50%

.. math::

   (\overrightarrow{AB} \times \overrightarrow{AP}) \cdot \mathbf{k}
      = |\overrightarrow{AB}| |\overrightarrow{AP}| \sin \phi_P
      = \left[ (b_1 - a_1) (p_2 - a_2) - (b_2 - a_2) (p_1 - a_1) \right]

   (\overrightarrow{AB} \times \overrightarrow{AQ}) \cdot \mathbf{k}
      = |\overrightarrow{AB}| |\overrightarrow{AQ}| \sin \phi_Q
      = \left[ (b_1 - a_1) (q_2 - a_2) - (b_2 - a_2) (q_1 - a_1) \right]

If :math:`\phi_P` and :math:`\phi_Q` have different signs, then
:math:`\overrightarrow{PQ}` crosses the line defined by :math:`\overline{AB}`.

Let :math:`\sigma(a)` denote the sign of a quantity :math:`a`.  Then the
line-crossing constraint is :math:`\sigma(\phi_P) \neq \sigma(\phi_Q)`.  Since
our angles are restricted to :math:`(-\pi, \pi)`, we can assume that
:math:`\sigma(\sin \phi) = \sigma(\phi)`.  And since vector amplitudes are
always positive, the line-crossing constraint reduces to these expressions.

.. math::

   \sigma_P = \sigma \left[
      (b_1 - a_1) (p_2 - a_2) - (b_2 - a_2) (p_1 - a_1)
   \right]

   \sigma_Q = \sigma \left[
      (b_1 - a_1) (q_2 - a_2) - (b_2 - a_2) (q_1 - a_1)
   \right]

where :math:`\sigma_X \equiv \sigma(\sin \phi_X)` for points :math:`X`.

A similar angle test can be used to determine whether the crossing occurs
within the length of the platform, as seen in the figure below.

.. image:: asy/along_platform.svg
   :align: center
   :width: 50%

The line along the displacement :math:`\overrightarrow{PQ}` crosses between the
segment endpoints of :math:`\overline{AB}` if :math:`\phi_A` and :math:`\phi_B`
are of opposite sign.  The corresponding sign expressions are

.. math::

   \sigma_A = \sigma \left[
      (q_1 - p_1) (a_2 - p_2) - (q_2 - p_2) (a_1 - p_1)
   \right]

   \sigma_B = \sigma \left[
      (q_1 - p_1) (b_2 - p_2) - (q_2 - p_2) (b_1 - p_1)
   \right]

which can be used to determine if :math:`\sigma_A \neq \sigma_B`.

.. \phi = \pi may be interesting for pathological cases, or just a
   mathematically interesting but irrelevant edge case.  Not sure yet.


Flat platforms
--------------

The simplest case to consider is flat boundaries.  For a horizontal platform,
the length is :math:`|b_1 - a_1|` and the height is :math:`a_2 = b_2`.

The platform-line crossing test reduces to a simple and expected result.

.. math::

   \sigma_P = \sigma(b_1 - a_1) \sigma(p_2 - a_2)

   \sigma_Q = \sigma(b_1 - a_1) \sigma(q_2 - a_2)

The :math:`\sigma_P \neq \sigma_Q` condition reduces to :math:`\sigma(p_2 -
a_2) \neq \sigma(q_2 - a_2)`; that is, :math:`p_2` and :math:`q_2` are on
opposite sides of the line which passes through :math:`a_2`.

This presumes a nonzero platform length.  A zero-length platform reduces the
collision target to a single point, which is often not the intention of the
developer.

Passing within the platform is less simple.  The only minor simplification is
the common offset term.

   \sigma_A = \sigma \left[
      (q_1 - p_1) (a_2 - p_2) - (q_2 - p_2) (a_1 - p_1)
   \right]

   \sigma_B = \sigma \left[
      (q_1 - p_1) (b_2 - p_2) - (q_2 - p_2) (b_1 - p_1)
   \right]


The first term in each expression is constant, but one is still required to
compute the full expressions.

Often, one does not try to compute the actual difference, and will instead use
an approximate check for whether either :math:`p_1` or :math:`q_1` is between
:math:`a_1` and :math:`b_1`.  This resembles the :math:`\sigma_P \neq \sigma_Q`
test and might be justified if the left-right displacement :math:`q_1 - p_1` is
small.
